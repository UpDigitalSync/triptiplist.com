Best Cities to Visit in Spain: Madrid, Barcelona, Seville & Valencia

I spent three weeks bouncing between these four cities by train and bus, eating my way through markets and crashing at a mix of boutique hotels and functional hostels. Here’s what actually worked, what didn’t, and where I’d send a friend on a first trip.

How many days should I spend in each city?

Four cities in two weeks is doable but tight. I’d give Madrid and Barcelona four full days each, Seville three, and Valencia two—plus travel days. That lets you hit the main sights without sprinting.

  • Madrid (4 days): Enough for the Prado, Retiro Park, a day trip to Toledo, and lazy tapas crawls in La Latina.
  • Barcelona (4 days): Gaudi sites, the Gothic Quarter, Montjuïc, and one beach afternoon.
  • Seville (3 days): Alcázar, Plaza de España, and flamenco in Triana.
  • Valencia (2 days): City of Arts and Sciences, the Central Market, and a paella lunch.

I found Seville’s smaller scale made three days feel generous; Valencia’s main draws are compact enough for a weekend.

What’s the best way to get between these cities?

High-speed AVE trains are the backbone. I booked through Renfe’s website and saved money by buying a week ahead. Barcelona to Madrid is about 2.5 hours; Madrid to Seville is the same. Valencia is the odd one out—it’s 1.5 hours from Madrid but 3 hours from Barcelona.

  • Madrid to Seville: AVE train, €45-70 one-way. Arrives at Santa Justa station, which is a 15-minute walk from the Alcázar.
  • Barcelona to Valencia: Euromed train, 3 hours. I used this and found the ride smooth, with coastal views near Tarragona.
  • Seville to Valencia: No direct high-speed. I took a bus (ALSA, 8 hours) and regretted it. Fly or go via Madrid.
  • Internal city transit: In Madrid, the Metro covers everything. In Seville, I walked—it’s flat and compact. Barcelona’s T-Casual card (10 rides for €11.35) worked perfectly.

Skip renting a car between cities. Parking is a nightmare in all four, and the train stations drop you right in the center.

What should I actually eat and where?

I ate very well and very cheaply. The key is avoiding the main squares and following locals into side streets.

  • Madrid: Casa Toni on Calle de la Cruz for grilled mushrooms and chorizo. Mercado de San Miguel is touristy but worth one pass for a glass of vermut. For proper cocido, La Bola on Calle de la Bola—book ahead.
  • Barcelona: El Xampanyet near the Picasso Museum for cava and anchovies. Canete for a splurge—get the tasting menu. Avoid La Rambla restaurants; the paella there is reheated.
  • Seville: Bar Europa on Calle San Eloy for spinach and chickpeas (espinacas con garbanzos). Bodega Santa Cruz for a cheap montadito and Cruzcampo. Triana’s Calle Betis has riverfront tapas bars.
  • Valencia: La Pepica on the beach for authentic paella—they cook it over wood fire. Central Market has a dozen stalls selling horchata and fartons.

One rule I followed: if the menu is in English, Spanish, and German, keep walking. If it’s handwritten in Spanish only, sit down.

Which neighborhoods should I stay in for convenience and value?

I stayed in four different areas and learned that a good location saves an hour a day.

  • Madrid: Sol or La Latina. Sol puts you near the Metro hub and Plaza Mayor. La Latina is quieter and full of tapas bars. I stayed at Room007 Chueca Hostel —clean, social, and €30/night for a private room in a great neighborhood.
  • Barcelona: El Born or Gràcia. El Born is central, with Gothic alleys and boutique shops. Gràcia is more local and relaxed, with fewer pickpockets. Hotel Rec Barcelona in El Born was quiet and well-priced.
  • Seville: Santa Cruz or Triana. Santa Cruz is the old Jewish quarter—beautiful but crowded. Triana across the river feels authentic and has better bars. Hotel Alminar in Santa Cruz was a good mid-range option.
  • Valencia: El Carmen or Ruzafa. El Carmen is the historic center; Ruzafa is up-and-coming with cool cafes. The Westin Valencia was a splurge but near the City of Arts.

Avoid the immediate area around Barcelona’s Plaça de Catalunya—it’s loud and full of scam artists at night.

What are the must-see attractions, and which are overrated?

I’ll be honest: some famous spots are worth the queue, and a few are skippable.

  • Worth the hype: The Alcázar of Seville (book tickets weeks ahead—the gardens are the highlight). Park Güell in Barcelona (go at 8am opening to avoid crowds). The Prado Museum in Madrid (focus on Goya’s Black Paintings and Velázquez’s Las Meninas).
  • Overrated: La Boqueria market in Barcelona—it’s a tourist zoo with €8 smoothies. Plaza Mayor in Madrid is just a big square with overpriced drinks. City of Arts and Sciences in Valencia is stunning from outside but the museums inside are hit-or-miss.
  • Hidden gems: Cerámica de Triana in Seville—a free workshop tour where you watch artisans paint tiles. Tabacalera in Madrid—a former tobacco factory turned street art space. L’Oceanogràfic in Valencia is actually excellent for families.

For Gaudi’s Sagrada Familia, I paid for the tower access and the Passion façade view. Worth it, but be prepared for construction noise.

When is the best time to visit these cities?

I went in late April and early May—perfect weather (20-25°C), fewer crowds than summer, and all sights were open. Here’s the breakdown:

  • Spring (March-May): Best overall. Seville’s Feria de Abril is in April, but book hotels six months out.
  • Summer (June-August): Seville and Madrid hit 40°C. Barcelona is packed with cruise crowds. Valencia is slightly cooler but still hot. I’d avoid unless you love heat.
  • Fall (September-November): Second best. September is warm but pleasant. Valencia’s Fallas festival is in March, not fall.
  • Winter (December-February): Cheap and quiet. Madrid has Christmas markets; Seville is mild (15°C). Some attractions have reduced hours.

I’d skip August entirely. I visited Madrid in August once and the city felt half-empty and sweltering.

FAQ

Is it safe to walk around these cities at night? Yes, with normal caution. I felt safe in all four after dark in central areas. Barcelona’s El Raval and Madrid’s Lavapiés can feel sketchy late—stay aware. Pickpocketing is the real risk, especially on Metro lines L3 and L5 in Barcelona. I kept my wallet in a front zippered pocket and had zero issues.

Can I do a day trip from Madrid or Barcelona? Yes. From Madrid, Toledo is 30 minutes by AVE—worth it for the cathedral and El Greco’s house. Segovia is an hour by bus and has a Roman aqueduct. From Barcelona, Montserrat is an hour by train and cable car—go for the monastery and mountain views. I did both and they were highlights.

Which city has the best nightlife? Madrid and Barcelona are tied, but different. Madrid’s nightlife starts late (bars at midnight, clubs at 2am) and centers on Malasaña and Chueca. Barcelona’s is more international, with beach clubs like Opium and Razzmatazz for live music. Seville’s is flamenco-focused—Casa de la Memoria has the best show. Valencia is more low-key; Mercado de Tapinería has good outdoor bars.

Conclusion

  • Madrid wins for museums and late-night food; stay in La Latina or Sol.
  • Barcelona is the most visually stunning but also the most tourist-trappy; avoid La Boqueria and watch your pockets.
  • Seville is the most charming and walkable; book Alcázar tickets early.
  • Valencia is the most underrated; go for the paella and the futuristic architecture.
  • Use AVE trains between cities, book ahead for savings, and skip August.
