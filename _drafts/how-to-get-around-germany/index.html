Getting Around Germany: Trains, Flights & Rental Cars

I landed in Berlin on a Tuesday morning with a rail pass, a rental car booked for the second week, and a stubborn belief that German efficiency would make everything easy. It mostly did — but not always in the way I expected. Here’s what actually worked and what didn’t when I crisscrossed Germany by train, plane, and car.

Is the Deutsche Bahn train network reliable enough for a multi-city trip?

Yes, but with caveats. The Deutsche Bahn (DB) network is extensive and fast. I took the ICE (Intercity-Express) from Berlin to Munich in about four hours, and the ICE from Munich to Hamburg in roughly five hours. The trains are clean, have WiFi (spotty in tunnels), and the DB Navigator app is essential for real-time platform changes.

But “pünktlich” (punctual) is aspirational. About one in three of my long-distance trains ran 10–20 minutes late, especially on the Frankfurt–Cologne corridor. For connections with tight transfers, I learned to leave at least 20 minutes. The BahnCard 25 saved me 25% on all fares — worth it if you take more than two long trips. For budget travelers, FlixTrain runs the same routes for less, though with fewer departures and no first class.

Should I rent a car or rely on public transit in cities?

In cities, skip the car. In the countryside, rent one. I picked up a rental from Sixt at Berlin Hauptbahnhof for a week-long trip through Bavaria, and it was the right call for visiting small towns like Rothenburg ob der Tauber and the Romantic Road. But driving into central Munich or Hamburg was a mistake — parking costs €25–€40 per day, and the Umweltzone (low-emission zone) requires a green sticker (Feinstaubplakette) on your windshield.

For city transit:

  • Berlin: The U-Bahn and S-Bahn cover everything. I used the BVG app for tickets. A day pass (€9.90) is cheaper than four single rides.
  • Munich: The MVV network is clean and logical. The U6 line takes you straight to the Olympiapark.
  • Hamburg: The HVV system includes ferries on the Elbe. Take the S1 from the airport to the Hauptbahnhof — it’s direct and faster than a taxi.

What’s the best way to get between Berlin, Munich, and Hamburg?

For speed, fly. For comfort and scenery, take the train. For budget, take the bus.

I flew Eurowings from Berlin to Munich once — 1 hour 15 minutes in the air, but with check-in and security at Berlin Brandenburg Airport (BER) , the door-to-door time was closer to four hours. The ICE train from Berlin to Munich took the same total time when you factor in airport hassle, and I could work and eat on board.

My preferred route:

  • Berlin → Hamburg: ICE train, 1 hour 45 minutes. Book a seat on the left side for views of the Alster lakes as you arrive.
  • Hamburg → Munich: ICE train, 5 hours. Long but not painful — the dining car has decent Currywurst and beer.
  • Munich → Berlin: FlixBus overnight for €19.99. It’s cramped but cheap, and the driver stopped for a 15-minute break at a Raststätte near Nuremberg.

Are domestic flights worth it for long distances?

Only for the extremes. A flight from Hamburg to Munich (1.5 hours) saves about 3.5 hours over the train, but you lose time getting to and from airports. BER is 30 minutes from central Berlin by RE7 regional train; Munich Airport (MUC) is 40 minutes on the S1 or S8.

I booked a Lufthansa flight from Berlin to Stuttgart once — €89 with a checked bag. The train would have been €120 and 6 hours. For that specific route, flying made sense. But for Berlin–Hamburg or Munich–Nuremberg, the train is faster door-to-door.

What about buses and ride-sharing?

FlixBus is everywhere. I took it from Munich to Neuschwanstein Castle (a two-hour ride for €9.99) and from Hamburg to Cologne (€14.99). The buses have WiFi, power outlets, and a toilet — but seats are tight and the AC can be weak in summer. Book the front row for legroom.

BlaBlaCar (ride-sharing) is popular in Germany. I used it once from Berlin to Leipzig — €12, a friendly driver named Klaus, and a faster trip than the train because we skipped the stops. The app is in English and works well.

Is the Deutschlandticket worth it for tourists?

The Deutschlandticket (€49 per month) gives you unlimited travel on all regional trains (RE, RB, S-Bahn) and local transit (U-Bahn, buses, trams) across Germany. It does not cover ICE, IC, or EC trains.

I bought it for a week and used it for:

  • Berlin to Potsdam on the RE1 (40 minutes)
  • Munich to Garmisch-Partenkirchen on the RE61 (1.5 hours)
  • Hamburg to Lübeck on the RE8 (1 hour)

For a tourist doing day trips from major cities, it’s excellent. But if you’re hopping between Berlin, Munich, and Hamburg on ICE trains, it’s useless — you still need separate long-distance tickets.

FAQ

Is it easy to use public transit in Germany without speaking German? Yes. All major transit apps (DB Navigator, BVG, MVV, HVV) have English interfaces. Ticket machines at stations default to English after the first button press. Signs on trains and platforms use symbols and abbreviations that are intuitive. I never had an issue.

Do I need to validate my train ticket before boarding? For regional and local transit tickets bought from machines, yes — stamp them in the small red or blue boxes on the platform. For tickets bought on the DB Navigator app or online, they’re already validated. Forgetting to validate can result in a €60 fine, even if you have a valid ticket.

Can I bring a bicycle on German trains? Yes, but with restrictions. On ICE trains, you need a bike reservation (€9) and space is limited — book early. On regional trains (RE, RB), bikes are allowed free or for a small fee, but avoid rush hour (7–9 AM and 4–6 PM) when they’re banned on some routes. On FlixBus, bikes cost €9 extra and go in the cargo hold.

Conclusion

  • Use the ICE train for Berlin–Hamburg and Munich–Nuremberg; fly for Hamburg–Munich if you value time over cost.
  • Rent a car from Sixt for Bavaria’s countryside, but leave it parked in cities — the U-Bahn in Berlin and Munich is faster and cheaper.
  • The Deutschlandticket pays for itself in three day trips; skip it if you only take ICE trains.
  • Book FlixBus for budget long-distance and BlaBlaCar for short hops between smaller cities.
  • Always carry the DB Navigator app, a backup power bank, and a printed backup of your ticket — I once lost signal in a tunnel and had to argue with a conductor.
