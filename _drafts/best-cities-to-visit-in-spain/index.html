Best Cities to Visit in Spain: Barcelona, Madrid, Seville

I spent three weeks bouncing between Barcelona, Madrid, and Seville last spring, and the differences between them hit me hard. Barcelona is loud and coastal, Madrid feels like a sprawling cultural machine, and Seville is slow and sun-baked. Each city demands a different approach—and a different packing strategy. Here’s what I learned, where I ate, and what I’d skip next time.

Why visit Barcelona first?

Barcelona hits you with Gaudí architecture and beach energy the moment you leave the airport. I landed at El Prat and took the Aerobus into Plaça de Catalunya—cheaper than a taxi, and the bus runs every five minutes. The city is walkable but sprawling, so I used the metro constantly. The L2 and L5 lines cover most tourist zones.

Places I’d go back to:

  • La Boqueria market on La Rambla—arrive before 10 a.m. to avoid the crush. Get a jamón ibérico cone from Pinotxo Bar.
  • Park Güell—book tickets online two days ahead. The ceramic bench mosaic is worth the uphill walk.
  • El Born neighborhood for evening tapas. Bar del Pla did a patatas bravas that ruined me for all others.
  • Barceloneta Beach—fine for a quick dip, but the sand is packed. Go early or skip it for a rooftop pool.

I stayed at Hotel Barcelona 1882 near the Sagrada Família. Quiet area, decent breakfast, and the staff pointed me to a hole-in-the-wall churrería called Churrería Laietana two blocks away. Avoid eating on La Rambla itself—everything there is overpriced and lukewarm.

Is Madrid better than Barcelona for culture?

Madrid doesn’t have a beach, but it has the Prado, the Reina Sofía, and a nightlife scene that starts at midnight. I took the AVE high-speed train from Barcelona Sants to Madrid Atocha—two hours, forty minutes. Book the train through Renfe at least two weeks ahead for half the price.

What I actually did in Madrid:

  • Prado Museum—go on a weekday afternoon. The Goya black paintings are in the basement and easy to miss. Don’t.
  • Mercado de San Miguel—touristy but worth one visit for the oyster stand and a glass of verdejo.
  • Retiro Park—rented a rowboat on the lake. Corny? Yes. Fun? Absolutely.
  • Lavapiés neighborhood for cheap, authentic Indian and Moroccan food. Café de la Luz has good coffee and a quiet courtyard.

I booked Room Mate Óscar in Chueca. Rooftop pool with a view of the Gran Vía, but the rooms are small. Better value: Petit Palace Chueca two blocks away—quieter, bigger beds, same neighborhood energy. Avoid the Plaza Mayor for dinner; everything is a trap. Walk to Calle Cava Baja instead and try Taberna La Dolores for grilled squid.

What makes Seville different from the other two?

Seville is smaller, hotter, and slower. I arrived by AVE from Madrid—two and a half hours—and stepped into dry, 38°C heat in June. The city center is compact; I walked everywhere. The orange trees lining every street smell incredible in spring, but the fruit is bitter. Don’t pick it.

Things that worked:

  • Real Alcázar—book the guided tour. The gardens are the highlight, not the palace rooms. I spent two hours just in the hedges.
  • Plaza de España—free and photogenic before 9 a.m. After that, it’s packed with selfie sticks.
  • Santa Cruz neighborhood—narrow alleys, whitewashed walls, and flamenco spilling out of doorways. Casa de la Memoria has an intimate flamenco show for €18.
  • Metropol Parasol (Las Setas)—controversial modern structure, but the sunset view from the top is unbeatable. Go at 8 p.m.

I stayed at Hotel Casa 1800 in Santa Cruz. Rooftop plunge pool, free sangria at check-in, and a room that opened onto a courtyard with a fountain. For food, El Rinconcillo is the oldest bar in Seville—cash only, order the spinach and chickpeas. Avoid La Carbonería for flamenco; it’s become a tourist cattle call.

When is the best time to visit each city?

Timing matters more in Spain than I expected. I hit Seville in June and regretted not going in April. Barcelona in May was perfect—warm enough for the beach, not yet a mob scene. Madrid was fine in late May, but the museums were crowded.

Seasonal breakdown:

  • Barcelona: April–June or September–October. July–August is humid and packed. November is rainy.
  • Madrid: March–May for mild weather and fewer crowds. December has Christmas markets and lower hotel rates.
  • Seville: March–April for Semana Santa or October for the Feria. Avoid July–August unless you like 42°C afternoons.

I used a local eSIM from Airalo for data across all three cities—cheaper than a roaming plan, no SIM swap needed. For insurance, I went with SafetyWing after a friend’s bag got lifted on the Madrid Metro. It paid out within a week.

How do you get between these cities without wasting time?

The AVE train network is the only way. I took three trains total: Barcelona to Madrid (2h40m), Madrid to Seville (2h30m), and Seville back to Barcelona (5h30m—long, but I slept). Book on Renfe’s official site and avoid third-party resellers. First-class (Preferente) gets you a meal and wider seats, but Turista is fine for the shorter legs.

Train station tips:

  • Barcelona Sants—arrive 20 minutes early. The platform boards change last-minute.
  • Madrid Atocha—has a tropical indoor garden with turtles. Yes, turtles. Walk through it.
  • Seville Santa Justa—small station, one café, limited seating. Grab food before you arrive.

I don’t recommend renting a car for this route. Parking in Barcelona’s Gothic Quarter is a nightmare, and Madrid’s M-30 ring road is confusing. Trains drop you in the city center every time.

What should you skip in each city?

I wasted time and money on a few things so you don’t have to.

Skip these:

  • Barcelona: The Casa Batlló audio tour—overpriced at €35 and the crowds are suffocating. Sagrada Família is worth it; Batlló isn’t.
  • Madrid: The Royal Palace if you’ve seen any European palace before. It’s all chandeliers and velvet ropes. Skip the changing of the guard too—it’s a 10-minute shuffle.
  • Seville: The flamenco shows on Calle Sierpes—factory-produced and €40 for a drink. Stick to Casa de la Memoria or a small tablao in Triana.

Also, don’t buy a Barcelona Card unless you plan to hit six museums in two days. I did the math—it didn’t pay off. Pay-as-you-go metro tickets worked fine.

FAQ

Is it safe to walk around Barcelona at night? Yes in most areas, but La Rambla after midnight gets sketchy. I saw three pickpocket attempts in one evening near the Liceu metro stop. Keep your phone in your front pocket or a crossbody bag with a zipper. The Gothic Quarter is fine if you stick to well-lit main streets. El Born and Gràcia felt safe even late.

Do I need to speak Spanish in Madrid? Not really, but it helps. English is common in museums, hotels, and tourist restaurants. In Lavapiés or local markets, I used basic phrases and Google Translate. Younger Madrileños speak good English. In Seville, English is less common—I learned “la cuenta, por favor” (the check, please) on day one and used it constantly.

How many days should I spend in each city? Three days in Barcelona, three in Madrid, two in Seville. That gives you time for one museum, one neighborhood walk, and one day-trip option per city. If you only have a week, pick Barcelona and Seville—they’re more distinct from each other. Madrid feels like a bonus city for repeat visitors.

Conclusion

  • Barcelona is for first-timers who want beach + architecture. Stay in El Born or Gràcia, not the Gothic Quarter.
  • Madrid rewards museum lovers and night owls. Base yourself in Chueca or Lavapiés for local energy.
  • Seville is the most charming but the hottest. Go in spring, book Alcázar tickets early, and eat at El Rinconcillo.
  • Use the AVE trains between cities. Book two weeks ahead for the best price.
  • Skip the tourist-trap flamenco shows and overpriced Rambla restaurants. Your wallet and your stomach will thank you.
