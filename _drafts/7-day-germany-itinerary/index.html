7 Days in Germany: Berlin, Munich & Frankfurt

I landed in Berlin with a vague plan and a train pass, and seven days later I’d hit three very different German cities without feeling like a hamster on a travel wheel. This itinerary skips the fluff—here’s exactly how to split a week between Berlin’s grit, Munich’s beer halls, and Frankfurt’s surprising edge, with real stops and honest opinions.

Is 7 days enough for Berlin, Munich, and Frankfurt?

Yes, if you move by train and don’t try to “do” all of Germany. I used the ICE high-speed rail between cities—three hours from Berlin to Frankfurt, then three more to Munich. That’s not wasted time; it’s a chance to watch the landscape shift from flat Brandenburg fields to the rolling hills of Bavaria. You lose half a day each travel day, but you gain three distinct German experiences.

My breakdown:

  • Berlin: 3 nights — enough for history, nightlife, and a slow morning in Prenzlauer Berg
  • Munich: 2 nights — tight but doable for the Altstadt, a beer hall, and a day trip to Dachau
  • Frankfurt: 1 night — honestly, one day is plenty unless you love finance districts

What should I do in Berlin for three days?

Berlin doesn’t care about your itinerary. It’s messy, loud, and full of layers. I started at Brandenburg Gate (touristy, yes, but walk through it at 8am when it’s quiet), then spent an afternoon at the Topography of Terror—a free outdoor museum on the site of the Gestapo headquarters. It’s more sobering than the overpriced Checkpoint Charlie photo-op.

Three Berlin musts:

  • Museum Island — buy a day pass and hit the Pergamon Museum (the Ishtar Gate inside is jaw-dropping), then skip the Neues Museum if you’re short on time
  • East Side Gallery — a 1.3km stretch of Berlin Wall murals, best visited on foot or by bike rental from Lidl Bike stations
  • Markthalle Neun in Kreuzberg — go Thursday evening for Street Food Thursday; I had a currywurst from Konnopke’s Imbiss that rewired my brain

For accommodation, I stayed at Hotel Amano near Rosenthaler Platz—great location, minimal design, and a rooftop bar that’s not just for Instagram. Skip the TV Tower queue; the view from the Panoramapunkt at Potsdamer Platz is cheaper and less crowded.

How do I get from Berlin to Munich, and what’s worth seeing along the way?

Take the ICE train from Berlin Hauptbahnhof to Munich Hauptbahnhof. It’s about four hours direct, but I stopped for a three-hour layover in Nuremberg. The old town is small enough to see in a couple of hours—walk through the Hauptmarkt, grab a bratwurst at Bratwursthäusle, and climb the Kaiserburg castle for a view of the red roofs. If you’re short on time, skip the Nazi rally grounds; the Documentation Center is powerful but heavy, and you’ve got Dachau coming up.

Train tips:

  • Book a seat reservation (€4.90) on the ICE—unreserved cars fill up fast
  • Use the DB Navigator app for real-time platform changes
  • First class is worth it for quieter carriages and free coffee, but second class is fine for a four-hour ride

What’s the best way to spend two days in Munich?

Munich feels like a clean, wealthy cousin to Berlin’s punk uncle. Day one is for the Altstadt: start at Marienplatz to watch the Glockenspiel (it’s kitschy, but the crowd energy is fun), then wander into Viktualienmarkt for a white sausage breakfast at Bräurosl. Skip the overpriced beer at the market stalls and head to Hofbräuhaus for the real experience—it’s a tourist trap, but the communal tables and oompah band make it worth one liter.

Day two:

  • Morning at Dachau Concentration Camp Memorial — take the S2 train from Munich Hbf to Dachau station, then bus 726. It’s a 20-minute ride. The site is free, but the audio guide (€4) is essential. Be prepared: it’s emotionally draining, but it’s the most important thing I did in Germany.
  • Afternoon in Schwabing — walk through the English Garden (bigger than Central Park), then grab a beer at the Chinesischer Turm beer garden. Locals pack it on sunny afternoons, and you can order a pretzel bigger than your head.

I stayed at Hotel Bayerischer Hof near the Hauptbahnhof—it’s a bit corporate, but the location is perfect for train connections. If you want more character, try Hotel Blauer Bock in the old town; it’s older and creakier, but the staff recommended a schnitzel spot called Augustiner-Keller that was the best meal of my trip.

Is Frankfurt worth visiting for one day?

Frankfurt gets a bad rap as a business city, and that’s fair—the skyline is mostly banks and hotels. But the Römerberg square is genuinely charming, and the Main Tower observation deck (€9) gives you a killer view of the city’s weird mix of medieval and modern. I spent the morning at the Städel Museum (world-class art, especially the German Expressionists), then walked along the Main River to the Eiserner Steg bridge.

What to skip:

  • The Zeil shopping street — it’s just a mall with more foot traffic
  • Sachsenhausen’s apple wine taverns — they’re fine, but you’ll pay tourist prices for a drink that’s mostly sour apple juice

For food, I found a gem: Kleinmarkthalle, a covered market near the Hauptbahnhof. Grab a Frankfurter Würstchen from Börse Frankfurt stand and a slice of Frankfurter Kranz cake from the bakery counter. I stayed at Hotel Villa Orange—a small boutique spot in the Westend neighborhood that felt more like a guesthouse than a chain.

Can I add a day trip to Hamburg or the Rhine Valley?

If you have extra time—say, you can stretch to 8 or 9 days—skip Hamburg (it’s a four-hour train from Berlin) and do a day trip from Frankfurt to the Rhine Valley. Take the regional train from Frankfurt Hbf to Rüdesheim, then hop a ferry across the river to Bingen. The stretch between Loreley Rock and St. Goar is the most scenic, with vineyards and castles every few hundred meters. It’s a full day, but it’s the kind of Germany you see in postcards—and it’s real.

FAQ

Is it better to fly between cities or take the train? Train, every time. ICE trains from Berlin to Munich take 4 hours door-to-door; flying adds airport security, transit to the airport, and delays. Plus, the train drops you in the city center. I used Deutsche Bahn and never had a delay longer than 15 minutes.

How much German do I need to know? Almost none in Berlin and Frankfurt—everyone under 40 speaks English. In Munich, older Bavarians at beer gardens might not, but pointing at a menu works. Learn “ein Bier, bitte” and “danke.” That’s it.

What’s the best time of year for this itinerary? May or September. June–August is packed with tourists and heat (Berlin has no AC in most hotels). November–March is cold and dark, but Christmas markets in Munich and Frankfurt are magical. I went in April—rainy but uncrowded.

Conclusion

  • Berlin needs 3 days — focus on history (Topography of Terror, Museum Island) and neighborhood walks (Kreuzberg, Prenzlauer Berg)
  • Munich works in 2 days — one for the Altstadt and Hofbräuhaus, one for Dachau and the English Garden
  • Frankfurt is a 1-day stop — Städel Museum, Main Tower, and Kleinmarkthalle market are the highlights
  • Trains are your best friend — ICE between cities, regional trains for day trips
  • Skip the tourist traps — TV Tower, Checkpoint Charlie, and Munich’s glockenspiel are fine in small doses, but don’t build your day around them
