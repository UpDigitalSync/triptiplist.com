Getting Around Norway: Trains, Ferries & Car Rentals

I’ll be straight with you: getting around Norway is expensive, but it’s also one of the most scenic parts of the trip if you plan it right. I spent two weeks bouncing between Oslo, Bergen, Tromsø, and Stavanger, and I learned that the right choice between train, ferry, and rental car depends entirely on where you’re going and what you want to see. Here’s what actually worked, what didn’t, and what I wish I’d known before booking.

Should you take the train between Oslo and Bergen?

Yes, absolutely—but only if you’re not in a rush. The Bergen Railway is a seven-hour ride, and I was skeptical at first, but it turned out to be one of the best travel days I’ve had. The train glides through the Hardangervidda plateau, and you get views of frozen lakes and snow-shed stations that you’d never see from a car. I booked a seat on the morning departure from Oslo S and arrived in Bergen just before dinner.

  • Oslo S is the main station; grab a coffee at Kaffebrenneriet before boarding.
  • The Myrdal stop is where you can transfer to the Flåm Railway—I’d skip it unless you have a full day to spare.
  • Bergen Station drops you right in the city center; our hotel Hotel Bergen Børs was a five-minute walk.
  • Book tickets on Vy’s website at least two weeks ahead for cheaper prices.

The train is comfortable with Wi-Fi, but the real payoff is the scenery. If you’re on a tight budget, the night train saves a hotel night, but you’ll miss the views.

Is the coastal ferry worth it in Tromsø?

In Tromsø, the ferry isn’t just transport—it’s the main event. I took the Tromsø–Senja ferry operated by Torghatten Nord, and it was the most practical way to see the fjords without a rental car. The trip takes about three hours, and you’ll pass through narrow channels with snow-capped mountains on both sides. I saw sea eagles circling above the boat.

  • Depart from Tromsø Prostneset terminal, right near the city center.
  • The Senja route is the most scenic; book a window seat on the upper deck.
  • Bring layers—the wind on deck is brutal even in summer.
  • Check the Hurtigruten coastal express if you want a multi-day option, but it’s pricier.

I’d skip the ferry if you’re prone to seasickness—the Norwegian Sea gets choppy. But for photography and sheer scale, it beats any bus tour.

When should you rent a car in Stavanger?

Rent a car in Stavanger only if you’re heading to the Preikestolen (Pulpit Rock) or the Lysefjord area. I made the mistake of renting one for city driving, and parking in downtown Stavanger is a nightmare—narrow streets and expensive lots. For the fjord trips, though, a car is essential.

  • Pick up from Avis or Hertz at Stavanger Airport—the city center offices have limited hours.
  • Drive to Preikestolen parking lot (about 45 minutes); the lot fills by 9 AM in summer.
  • The Ryfylke Tunnel is the world’s deepest undersea road tunnel—it’s eerie but fine.
  • Fill up at Circle K stations; rural gas stations are sparse and expensive.

I paid about 800 NOK per day for a compact car, which is steep, but it let me stop at Øvre Holmegate for a beer at Bøker og Børst without worrying about bus schedules. If you’re only staying in Stavanger city, skip the car and walk—it’s compact and walkable.

What’s the best way to get around Oslo?

Oslo is the one city where public transport is genuinely easy. I used the Ruter app for trams, buses, and the metro, and I never waited more than ten minutes. The T-bane (metro) is fast and covers all major neighborhoods, from Grünerløkka to Frogner.

  • Buy a 24-hour pass (117 NOK) from the Ruter app or at Oslo S.
  • The Tram 12 runs from Aker Brygge to Grünerløkka—great for bar-hopping.
  • The Ekebergbanen tram line goes up to Ekebergparken for a killer city view.
  • Avoid taxis—they’re absurdly expensive. I paid 400 NOK for a five-minute ride once.

I walked most of the time, but the tram saved my feet after a long dinner at Maaemo (yes, it’s worth the hype). The metro to Holmenkollen is also a no-brainer for the ski jump.

Are ferries useful in Bergen?

Bergen is a ferry city, and the Bybanen light rail handles the land side. I took the Bergen–Fløibanen funicular for the view, but the real workhorse was the Bergen–Askøy ferry for a day trip to the Askøy islands. It’s cheap, frequent, and runs year-round.

  • The Skyss app sells tickets for all ferries and buses.
  • The Fløibanen funicular is touristy but worth it for the sunset from Fløyen.
  • For a longer trip, the Hurtigbåt (express boat) to Rosendal is a solid day out.
  • The Bryggen wharf area has a ferry stop right at the historic buildings.

I’d skip the Fjord Tours packages—they’re overpriced and you can DIY the same routes with a Skyss pass. The ferry to Ulriken is a better hike than the funicular, in my opinion.

FAQ

Is it cheaper to book train tickets in advance or on the day? Book in advance. I paid 500 NOK for Oslo–Bergen on Vy’s Minipris deal two weeks out; the same ticket was 1,200 NOK at the station. For ferries, walk-up prices are the same, but car ferries need a reservation in summer.

Can you drive the Atlantic Road without a rental car? Not easily. The Atlantic Road is near Kristiansund, and there’s no direct bus. I’d only do it as a road trip from Trondheim or Ålesund. If you’re set on it, rent a car in Ålesund and budget two days for the loop.

Are electric cars common for rental in Norway? Yes, and they’re cheaper on tolls. I rented a Nissan Leaf from Europcar in Stavanger and saved about 200 NOK on the Ryfylke Tunnel toll. Just check the charging network—Tesla Superchargers are everywhere, but other EVs need a Circle K or Ionity card.

Conclusion

  • The Bergen Railway is the best scenic value for long-distance travel—book early.
  • Ferries in Tromsø and Bergen are practical and scenic, but skip them if you get seasick.
  • Rental cars are worth it only for fjord trips from Stavanger; avoid them in Oslo.
  • Oslo’s public transport is reliable and cheap—use the Ruter app.
  • Bergen’s ferries are DIY-friendly; skip the packaged tours.
