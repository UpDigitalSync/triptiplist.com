Best Day Trips from Seville: Ronda, Cádiz & Córdoba

I spent a week based in Seville and took three day trips that actually worked without feeling rushed. Here’s what I learned about getting to Ronda, Cádiz, and Córdoba—and what’s worth your time once you arrive.

Is Ronda worth the two-hour bus ride?

Yes, but only if you go for the gorge. Ronda’s Puente Nuevo bridge spanning the El Tajo canyon is the real draw. I took an early morning bus from Seville’s Plaza de Armas station (about two hours, direct). The town itself is compact—you can see the key sights in four hours.

  • Puente Nuevo – Walk across and then take the path down into the gorge for the classic angle. Free.
  • Plaza de Toros de Ronda – One of Spain’s oldest bullrings. The museum inside is dry, but the arena itself is impressive. Entry is €8.
  • Alameda del Tajo – A park with cliffside viewpoints. Good for a quick photo stop.
  • Restaurant Pedro Romero – Solid tapas near the bullring. I had the rabo de toro (oxtail stew) and it was better than any I’d had in Seville.

The bus back to Seville leaves around 5 p.m., so you won’t be stuck. Skip the guided tours that bundle Ronda with white villages—you’ll spend more time on the road than seeing anything.

How do you get to Cádiz, and is it worth the train ride?

The train from Seville’s Santa Justa station to Cádiz is the best day-trip option in this entire list. It takes 1 hour 40 minutes on the Renfe Media Distancia, costs about €25 round trip, and drops you right at Cádiz’s old town edge. No bus, no transfer.

Cádiz feels like a smaller, saltier, less crowded version of Seville. The old town is a peninsula ringed by water. I walked the entire city wall circuit in under two hours.

  • Torre Tavira – Climb this 18th-century watchtower for the best panoramic view. The cámara oscura show inside is actually interesting—they project a live image of the city onto a concave dish. Entry €7.
  • Playa de la Caleta – The city beach. Tiny by resort standards, but it’s between two castles and has real local character. I swam here in April—cold but fine.
  • Mercado Central de Abastos – Go for the fried fish. Grab a plate of pescaíto frito from the stall called Freiduría Las Flores. Eat standing at the bar.
  • Barrio de la Viña – The neighborhood for tapas. Try El Faro de Cádiz for tortillitas de camarones (shrimp fritters).

One warning: the train back to Seville after 6 p.m. can fill up. Book your return ticket in advance on the Renfe website.

Can you really see Córdoba in a day from Seville?

Yes, and it’s the easiest trip on this list. The AVE high-speed train from Santa Justa to Córdoba takes 45 minutes. I caught the 8:30 a.m. train, had coffee in Córdoba by 9:15, and was at the Mezquita before the tour groups arrived.

The Mezquita-Catedral is the main event. Book your ticket online the night before—the queue at the door can hit 30 minutes by 10 a.m. The ticket costs €11. Inside, the red-and-white horseshoe arches stretch in every direction. The Christian cathedral plopped in the middle is jarring, but that’s the point.

  • Mezquita-Catedral – Allow 90 minutes inside. Go early.
  • Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos – The gardens are better than the palace. Fountains, orange trees, and a moat. Entry €4.50.
  • Judería (Jewish Quarter) – Narrow whitewashed alleys. Calleja de las Flores is the famous one—it’s a photo spot, not a destination.
  • Patio de los Naranjos – Free courtyard attached to the Mezquita. Smells like orange blossom.
  • Taberna La Viuda – A small bar near the Mezquita. I had salmorejo (thick cold tomato soup) and it was the best I ate in Andalusia. €8 for a bowl and a beer.

Córdoba’s old town is walkable. You don’t need a tour. By 3 p.m., I was done with sightseeing and sitting in a plaza with a tinto de verano. The 4:30 p.m. AVE got me back to Seville by 5:15.

Which day trip is best for food?

Cádiz, hands down. Seville has great tapas, but Cádiz’s seafood is a different league. The city sits on the Atlantic, so the catch is fresher and cheaper. I ate ortiguillas (sea anemones fried in batter) at a bar called El Pópulo for €5—weird but delicious.

In Ronda, food is secondary to the views. Córdoba’s salmorejo is excellent, but you can find it in Seville too. If you have one day for eating, pick Cádiz.

Should you drive or take the train?

Train, every time. I rented a car for one day trip and regretted it. Parking in Ronda’s old town is a nightmare—I spent 20 minutes circling for a spot. Cádiz has a massive underground garage near the train station, but it costs €18 for a few hours. The AVE to Córdoba is faster than driving and drops you right at the old town.

The only exception is if you want to visit the white villages (pueblos blancos) like Grazalema or Zahara de la Sierra on the way to Ronda. Those require a car. But for Ronda, Cádiz, and Córdoba as standalone day trips, the train or bus is simpler.

FAQ

What is the cheapest day trip from Seville? Córdoba. The AVE round trip costs about €30 if you book a few days in advance. Cádiz is similar by Media Distancia train. Ronda is cheapest by bus—€20 round trip from Plaza de Armas.

Can you combine two day trips in one day? No. I tried to do Córdoba and Ronda in one day and ended up with three hours of travel and one hour of sightseeing. Pick one city per day.

Do I need to book tickets in advance for the Mezquita or Alcázar? For the Mezquita, yes. Book online the night before to skip the line. For the Alcázar in Córdoba, you can buy at the door. For Cádiz’s Torre Tavira, no advance booking needed.

Conclusion

  • Take the AVE to Córdoba for the Mezquita and salmorejo—it’s the fastest trip.
  • Take the Media Distancia train to Cádiz for seafood and seaside walking.
  • Take the bus to Ronda only for the gorge—skip the bullring if you’re short on time.
  • Don’t drive. Trains from Santa Justa are faster, cheaper, and less stressful.
  • Book return tickets in advance for Cádiz and Córdoba trains.
