Best UK Beaches: Cornwall, Devon & Scottish Isles Guide

I’ve spent the last three summers crisscrossing the UK coastline in a rented VW camper, chasing decent weather and decent swimming. The beaches here are not tropical — expect wind, unpredictable tides, and water that will make you gasp. But when the sun hits, there’s nowhere better. This guide covers three very different stretches: the sheltered coves of Cornwall, the long sandy shores of Bournemouth, and the raw, dramatic coastline of the Scottish Isles. I’ll tell you where to go, what to skip, and how to avoid the worst of the crowds.

What makes Cornwall’s beaches worth the drive?

Cornwall is the gold standard for UK beach holidays, but it comes with a price — traffic. The A30 crawls on summer weekends, and parking fills by 9 AM. I learned this the hard way trying to get into St Ives on a July Saturday. Park at the Lelant Saltings park-and-ride and take the train in. It saves an hour of queuing.

Porthminster Beach is the safest bet in St Ives for families — sheltered, clean sand, and the Porthminster Beach Café does a decent seafood platter. For surf, head east to Gwithian Towans, a three-mile stretch of dunes with consistent waves. Avoid Porthcurno on peak days unless you arrive before 8 AM; the Minack Theatre crowds spill onto the beach. If you want quiet, walk the South West Coast Path from Sennen Cove to Land’s End — you’ll find small coves like Nanjizal that are almost empty even in August.

  • Porthminster Beach — calm water, café, easy access from St Ives station
  • Gwithian Towans — best surf break in West Cornwall, free parking at Godrevy
  • Nanjizal Cove — requires a 20-minute coastal walk, no facilities, worth it
  • Porthcurno — stunning but overrun; go at low tide for more space

Is Bournemouth Beach overrated?

Yes and no. The seven miles of golden sand between Bournemouth and Boscombe are genuinely impressive — wide, clean, and with a promenade that stretches the whole way. The water is warmer than Cornwall (Gulf Stream effect), and the pier has the usual arcades and fish-and-chip shops. But it’s a resort town, not a hidden gem. Expect deckchair rentals, ice cream vans every 100 metres, and crowds on any day above 20°C.

What saves it for me is the walk from Bournemouth Pier to Alum Chine. It’s less packed than the central strip, and the chines (wooded ravines that cut down to the beach) provide shade and a bit of shelter from the wind. I stayed at the Hotel Miramar on East Overcliff — it’s dated but the sea views are unbeatable, and it’s a five-minute walk to the beach. For food, skip the pier stalls and walk up to Westbourne for pizza at Pomodoro. If you want quieter water, head east to Southbourne Beach — rock pools at low tide, fewer families, and a decent beach café called The Beach House.

  • Bournemouth Pier to Alum Chine — best stretch for a long walk, fewer crowds
  • Southbourne Beach — rock pools, quieter, good for a relaxed afternoon
  • Hotel Miramar — cliff-top location, old-school charm, direct sea views
  • Pomodoro Westbourne — proper Neapolitan pizza, 15-minute walk from the beach

What are the best beaches on the Isle of Skye?

Skye’s beaches are not for sunbathing. They are for dramatic walks, photography, and feeling small against the landscape. The sand is often black or pale grey, the water is Arctic-cold, and the wind rarely stops. That said, Talisker Bay is one of the most beautiful places I’ve ever seen — a crescent of black sand framed by basalt cliffs, with a waterfall at the north end. The walk from the car park is 15 minutes through a muddy field; wear boots.

Fairy Pools are wildly overrated in my opinion. The water is freezing, the path is crowded, and the photos you see online are heavily edited. Skip it and drive to Claigan Coral Beach instead. The “coral” is actually crushed limestone, but the water is turquoise on a sunny day, and the walk along the coast to the beach is spectacular. Stay at the Cuillin Hills Hotel in Portree for the best location — it’s pricey but the breakfast spread is worth it.

  • Talisker Bay — black sand, waterfall, basalt cliffs; go at low tide
  • Claigan Coral Beach — turquoise water, limestone sand, quieter than Fairy Pools
  • Cuillin Hills Hotel — Portree, great views, solid breakfast
  • Fairy Pools — skip unless you want Instagram photos and cold feet

When is the best time to visit UK beaches?

June and September are the sweet spots. July and August have the best weather odds, but also the worst crowds and highest prices. I’ve had perfect beach days in early June on St Ives’ Porthmeor Beach with almost no one around. September water temperatures are still tolerable (around 16-18°C in the south), and the light is softer for photos.

For the Scottish Isles, aim for late May to mid-June. The midges haven’t emerged yet, the days are long (sunset after 10 PM), and the tourist numbers are manageable. Avoid August in Skye unless you enjoy queuing for single-track roads. In Bournemouth, the air show in late August is chaos — beaches are packed, and parking is a nightmare. Come in September instead.

  • June — best balance of weather and crowds in Cornwall and Bournemouth
  • Late May — ideal for Skye before midges and crowds
  • September — warm water, fewer people, cheaper accommodation
  • Avoid August — peak crowds, traffic, and inflated prices everywhere

How do you handle parking and tides?

Parking is the single biggest headache for UK beach trips. In Cornwall, the Parkopedia app is essential. I use it to find free parking spots near Gwithian (Godrevy car park is free but fills by 8 AM). In St Ives, the Lelant Saltings train is cheaper and faster than driving into town. Bournemouth has several multi-storey car parks — the one at the BIC is central and costs about £12 for the day.

Tides matter more than you think. At low tide, beaches like Porthcurno triple in size, and rock pools appear. At high tide, some beaches (like Talisker Bay) shrink to almost nothing. Download the Tides Near Me app and check before you go. I once got stranded on a sandbar at Southbourne because I didn’t check — not fun.

  • Parkopedia app — find free or cheap parking near beaches
  • Lelant Saltings park-and-ride — best option for St Ives
  • Tides Near Me app — check high and low tide times
  • Always carry wellies or water shoes — rocky exits are common

What should you pack for a UK beach day?

The weather changes every 20 minutes. I pack a dryrobe, a windproof jacket, and a wool hat even in July. Sunscreen is non-negotiable — the wind hides UV burn, and I’ve seen lobster-red tourists on Bournemouth pier too many times. A picnic is smarter than relying on beach cafés, which often close by 4 PM or charge £8 for a sandwich.

For swimming, a wetsuit is recommended outside of July and August. I use a 3/2mm shorty for Cornwall in summer, and a full 5/4mm for Skye year-round. Booties help on rocky beaches like Talisker. And bring cash — some remote car parks in Skye don’t take cards.

  • Dryrobe or windproof jacket — essential for changing and shelter
  • Wetsuit — 3/2mm for south, 5/4mm for Scotland
  • Sunscreen and wool hat — windburn and sunburn are both real
  • Cash — for remote car parks and small cafés

FAQ

Are UK beaches safe for swimming? Yes, but always check local signage for rip currents and water quality. Cornwall’s beaches are generally clean (Blue Flag status on many). Bournemouth has lifeguards in summer. Skye’s beaches are wild — swim only in calm conditions and never alone. The water is cold enough to cause cold-water shock, so enter slowly.

Can I bring my dog to UK beaches? Many beaches have seasonal dog bans from May to September. In Cornwall, Porthminster and Porthcurno ban dogs in summer. Bournemouth’s central beach also restricts dogs. Southbourne and Alum Chine allow dogs year-round. On Skye, most beaches are dog-friendly, but keep them on a lead near livestock.

What’s the best beach for beginners to surf? Gwithian Towans in Cornwall is ideal — consistent waves, sandy bottom, and surf schools at Godrevy. Bournemouth has smaller waves better for longboarding. Skye is not a surfing destination; the waves are inconsistent and the water is dangerously cold.

Conclusion

  • Cornwall wins for variety and scenery — hit Gwithian for surf, Nanjizal for solitude, and Porthminster for easy access
  • Bournemouth is best for families and long beach walks — stick to Southbourne or Alum Chine to avoid crowds
  • Skye is for the adventurous — Talisker Bay and Claigan Coral Beach are unforgettable, but pack for wind and cold
  • Timing is everything — June and September are ideal; August is a traffic nightmare
  • Plan for parking and tides — these two factors make or break a UK beach day
