Best Beaches in Thailand: Phuket, Krabi & Koh Samui

I’ve spent the last three winters hopping between Thailand’s three big beach hubs. Phuket, Krabi, and Koh Samui each have their own vibe, and the “best” beach depends entirely on what you want. This guide cuts through the postcard hype and tells you where to actually put your towel.

Which beaches in Phuket are worth your time?

Phuket is huge, and its beaches vary wildly. Patong is a circus—loud, packed, and the water is often murky. I’d skip it unless you want nightlife steps from the sand. Instead, head north.

Kata Beach is my pick for a balanced stay. The southern end has decent waves for bodyboarding, and the main strip has solid food without the full Patong chaos. I ate at The Boathouse Wine & Grill one evening—pricey but the sunset view over Kata Noi is worth it.

Bang Tao Beach stretches for miles. The northern section near Trisara is quieter, while the middle (near Laguna Phuket) has resorts but also public access points. The water is calm—good for families.

Freedom Beach is only accessible by longtail boat from Patong or a steep jungle trail. It’s small, no vendors, and the snorkeling is average. Go early before the tour groups arrive.

Surin Beach has soft sand and a local market at the south end. The rip current can be strong—pay attention to the flags.

For a day trip, take a speedboat to Koh Phi Phi or Similan Islands (closed mid-Oct to mid-May). The water clarity there beats anything on Phuket’s west coast.

What are the best beaches in Krabi for swimming and scenery?

Krabi is more laid-back than Phuket. The mainland beaches (Ao Nang, Railay) are stunning but tidal. The islands (Koh Lanta, Koh Jum) are quieter.

Railay Beach is the crown jewel. You can only get there by boat from Ao Nang. East Railay is muddy at low tide—stick to West Railay for swimming. The limestone cliffs behind it are world-class for climbing. I booked a half-day climb with King Climbers and the views from the top were better than any beach photo.

Ao Nang Beach itself is convenient but not great for swimming—too many longtail boats. Use it as a base. Walk to the end of the beach and take a 10-minute trail to Phra Nang Cave Beach. That’s where the clear water and soft sand are. There’s a small shrine inside the cave with carved wooden phalluses—locals leave offerings for good luck.

Koh Lanta is a two-hour ferry from Ao Nang. Long Beach has shallow water that stays warm until late afternoon. I stayed at Lanta Sand Resort—right on the sand, affordable, and the Thai green curry at their restaurant was the best I had on the island.

Hong Islands (a short longtail trip from Ao Nang) have a lagoon you can swim in at high tide. The main beach is tiny but the water is emerald. Bring snacks—the one vendor there charges triple.

Tubkaek Beach is quiet, with views of the Hong Islands. It’s rocky in spots, so water shoes help. The Tubkaek Sunset Resort has a nice beachfront bar.

Which Koh Samui beaches deliver on the hype?

Koh Samui is more developed than Krabi but less chaotic than Phuket. The east coast has the best sand. The north coast has the sunsets.

Chaweng Beach is the longest and busiest. The water is clear near the central area, but the northern end (near Chaweng Regent Beach Resort) is quieter. The southern end has rocks. Nightlife is concentrated on Soi Green Mango—loud, but you can walk right back to the beach.

Lamai Beach is smaller and less polished. The sand is coarser, but the vibe is more relaxed. Grandfather and Grandmother Rocks (Hin Ta Hin Yai) are a five-minute walk from the south end—touristy but good for a photo.

Bophut Beach is my favorite on Samui. The Fisherman’s Village has a walking street market every Friday evening with decent street food. I ate grilled squid and mango sticky rice from a stall run by an older lady who’s been there for 15 years. The beach itself is shallow and calm—good for floating.

Mae Nam Beach is where you go to escape. Fewer resorts, more palm trees. The water is clear but the bottom is a bit weedy. W Koh Samui has a infinity pool overlooking the beach if you want a splurge.

Silver Beach (also called Crystal Bay) is a tiny cove between Chaweng and Lamai. The snorkeling is decent—I saw a few parrotfish and a small puffer. It’s public access, so arrive early to claim a spot.

When is the best time to visit each beach destination?

Thailand has three weather zones, and the seasons don’t line up.

Phuket (west coast) is best from November to April. The Andaman Sea is calm, skies are clear. May to October brings monsoon—rough seas and rain. I visited in late October once and got three days of downpour.

Krabi has the same window. November to March is prime. April gets hot (35°C+). June to September is rainy but cheaper. I went in July and had two sunny days out of seven. Still, the beaches were empty.

Koh Samui (Gulf coast) is opposite. Best weather is February to April. May to August sees short but heavy showers. September to November is the monsoon—flights get canceled, ferries stop running. I got stuck on Samui for an extra day in October due to a storm.

For all three, avoid April if you hate heat. December and January are peak season—book accommodation months ahead.

How do I get between Phuket, Krabi, and Koh Samui?

You have three options: ferry, bus-ferry combo, or flight.

Ferry: From Phuket (Rassada Pier) to Koh Samui takes 4–5 hours. The Lomprayah catamaran is the most reliable. From Krabi (Ao Nang or Klong Jilad Pier) to Koh Samui is similar. Ferries run daily but can be canceled in bad weather.

Bus-ferry combo: Cheaper but slower. From Phuket to Krabi, you take a bus to the pier, then a ferry. Total time is about 6 hours. I did this once—saved 400 baht but lost half a day.

Flight: Bangkok Airways flies between Phuket and Koh Samui (1 hour). It’s expensive (around $100 one-way) but worth it if you’re short on time. Air Asia flies from Krabi to Phuket for $30.

My advice: base yourself in one region and do day trips. Hopping islands every night is exhausting and eats up beach time.

What should I pack for a Thailand beach trip?

Beyond the obvious (swimsuit, sunscreen), here’s what I actually used:

  • Water shoes: Rocky entry at many beaches (Railay, Tubkaek, Silver Beach). Cheap ones from Decathlon work fine.
  • Dry bag: For phone and wallet on longtail boats. I use a Sea to Summit 20-liter.
  • Reef-safe sunscreen: The coral here is already stressed. Brands like Sun Bum are available in 7-Eleven on Samui.
  • Mosquito repellent: Especially at sunset on Samui and Krabi. The Soffell spray from local shops works better than imported brands.
  • eSIM: I use Airalo for data. No SIM card swapping, just scan a QR code. Coverage was solid on all three islands.

FAQ

Which beach has the clearest water in Thailand? Railay Beach West (Krabi) and Silver Beach (Koh Samui) have the clearest water I saw. For absolute clarity, take a day trip to the Similan Islands from Phuket—visibility can hit 30 meters. But they’re closed half the year.

Are the beaches in Thailand crowded? Patong (Phuket) and Chaweng (Samui) are packed December to February. Go to Tubkaek (Krabi) or Mae Nam (Samui) for empty sand. I had Mae Nam almost to myself on a weekday in March.

Can I swim at all these beaches year-round? No. Rip currents are common on Phuket’s west coast during monsoon (May–Oct). On Samui, jellyfish appear in the water during certain months—check local signs. Always swim where lifeguards are present.

Conclusion

  • Phuket is best for variety and nightlife. Skip Patong, head to Kata or Bang Tao.
  • Krabi has the most dramatic scenery. Railay and Koh Lanta are the highlights.
  • Koh Samui is the most convenient for families. Bophut and Mae Nam offer calm water and good food.
  • Go November to March for reliable weather across all three.
  • Pack water shoes and an eSIM. Book ferries in advance during peak season.
